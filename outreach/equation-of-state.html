The Equation of State of the Universe

From Newton's apple to an accelerating cosmos, in one number

Every ingredient of the universe has its own ratio of pressure to energy density, called \(w\). That one number fixes how fast the ingredient thins out as space expands, and whether its gravity pulls the expansion back or pushes it forward. Most of the machinery needed to see this is Newton's law of gravity plus the first law of thermodynamics. The part Newton could not supply is the reason the universe is accelerating today.

How the page runs. The first half derives the three equations that govern the expansion, starting from a ball of dust and nothing more than \(F = GMm/r^2\). The second half puts the equation of state into them. There are two interactive panels. One lets you dial \(w\) for a universe containing a single ingredient. The other shows the real mixture, with a switch between a cosmological constant and the evolving dark energy that DESI's data lean toward.

Parameters: Planck Collaboration, Planck 2018 results VI, A&A 641, A6 (2020): \(H_0 = 67.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), \(\Omega_\mathrm{m} = 0.315\). Dark energy: DESI Collaboration, DESI DR2 results II, arXiv:2503.14738.

A ball of dust

Fill space with a uniform, pressureless “dust” of density \(\rho(t)\), and let it expand uniformly, which means every distance grows by the same factor. Write the physical distance of a particle from any chosen centre as

\[ R(t) = a(t)\, x , \tag{1} \]

where \(x\) is a fixed label for that particle, its comoving distance, and \(a(t)\) is the scale factor, normalised to \(a = 1\) today. The expansion rate is the Hubble parameter, \(H \equiv \dot a/a\).

Pick any point as the centre and draw a sphere through a test particle of mass \(m\) at radius \(R\). Newton's shell theorem says a uniform spherical shell exerts no net force on anything inside it, so all the mass outside the sphere drops out. Only the mass inside pulls:

\[ M = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\,\rho\,R^3 . \tag{2} \]

No dust crosses the surface, because everything moves with the flow, so \(M\) stays constant even as \(\rho\) falls and \(R\) grows.

Newton's second law for the test particle is

\[ \ddot R = -\frac{GM}{R^2}. \tag{3} \]

Multiply both sides by \(\dot R\) and integrate once. The left side becomes the time derivative of \(\tfrac12\dot R^2\) and the right side the time derivative of \(GM/R\), so what comes out is energy conservation per unit mass:

\[ \tfrac12 \dot R^2 - \frac{GM}{R} = U = \text{constant}. \tag{4} \]

Now substitute \(R = ax\) and \(M = \tfrac43\pi\rho a^3x^3\), and divide through by \(\tfrac12 a^2x^2\):

\[ \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho + \frac{2U}{a^2 x^2}. \]

The left side and the first term on the right are the same for every particle in the universe. For the expansion to stay uniform, \(U/x^2\) has to be the same for every particle too. Call that constant \(-kc^2/2\), and the result is the Friedmann equation:

\[ H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho - \frac{k c^2}{a^2} . \tag{5} \]

This is exactly the equation general relativity gives for a homogeneous, isotropic universe. Friedmann found it in 1922 by solving Einstein's equations. Milne and McCrea showed in 1934 that Newton gets there too. What relativity changes is not the equation but what the symbols mean.

Why the sphere is allowed. The shell theorem is cleanly true for a finite ball, and slightly ambiguous for infinite space, where the “mass outside” is infinite. The rigorous version is Birkhoff's theorem in general relativity, which says the same thing: a spherically symmetric distribution of mass outside a sphere has no effect inside it. That is what really licenses the argument.

What the constant \(k\) is

To Newton, \(k\) is just the sign of the energy \(U\), and its meaning is the familiar one for a ball thrown upward. In relativity the same constant is the curvature of space. The fates agree, at least for dust.

Setting \(k = 0\) defines the critical density, \(\rho_\mathrm{c} = 3H^2/8\pi G\). With \(H_0 = 67.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\) it is \(8.5 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), about five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre. Dividing (5) by \(H^2\) and writing each density as a fraction \(\Omega = \rho/\rho_\mathrm{c}\) turns the Friedmann equation into a budget: the \(\Omega\)s plus a curvature term add up to one. The curvature term is measured to be below about 0.002 today, so from here on the universe is flat and \(k = 0\).

Adding thermodynamics

The Friedmann equation gives \(H\) once you know \(\rho\), but says nothing about how \(\rho\) changes. For dust it is easy: the mass in a comoving sphere is fixed, so \(\rho \propto a^{-3}\). For anything with pressure, the first law of thermodynamics is needed.

Take a comoving sphere of volume \(V \propto a^3\) holding energy \(E = \rho c^2 V\). In a uniform universe there is nowhere for heat to flow, so the expansion is adiabatic and

\[ dE = -P\,dV . \tag{6} \]

Write this as a rate, using \(\dot V/V = 3\dot a/a\):

\[ \dot\rho + 3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho + \frac{P}{c^2}\right) = 0 . \tag{7} \]

This is the fluid equation. A gas with positive pressure does work on its surroundings as it expands, so its energy density falls faster than its volume grows. The \(P\) term is that work and nothing more.

Mass quietly becomes energy. In this step \(\rho\) changes meaning. It is now the total energy density divided by \(c^2\), including heat and radiation, not just the rest mass of the dust. Newton has no reason to do this, but relativity insists on it, because all energy gravitates.

Pressure gravitates

Newton's law (3) on its own gives

\[ \frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\,\rho \qquad(\text{dust only}), \]

so gravity always slows the expansion. Now differentiate the Friedmann equation (5) with respect to time, use the fluid equation (7) to replace \(\dot\rho\), and use (5) once more to tidy up. The \(k\) term cancels, and the result is the acceleration equation:

\[ \frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3P}{c^2}\right) . \tag{8} \]

The key result

Pressure is a source of gravity, and it counts three times over. Ordinary pressure makes gravity stronger, not weaker. If something has a pressure more negative than \(-\tfrac13\rho c^2\), the bracket in (8) changes sign and the expansion speeds up. Newtonian gravity has no way to produce this. Yet putting Newton's Friedmann equation together with the first law of thermodynamics gets it automatically. In general relativity the reason is that the source of gravity is \(\rho + 3P/c^2\), not \(\rho\) alone.

Only two of (5), (7) and (8) are independent: any two give the third. That is why this derivation worked.

The equation of state

One more relation is needed to close the system, between pressure and density. For every ingredient that matters in cosmology it is a straight proportion,

\[ P = w\,\rho c^2 , \tag{9} \]

and the dimensionless number \(w\) is the equation of state. Put it into the fluid equation (7) and integrate:

\[ \rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)} . \tag{10} \]

Put that into the flat Friedmann equation and integrate again:

\[ a(t) \propto t^{\,2/[3(1+w)]} \quad (w > -1), \qquad a(t) \propto e^{Ht} \quad (w = -1). \tag{11} \]

And the acceleration equation (8) becomes \(\ddot a/a = -\tfrac43\pi G\rho\,(1 + 3w)\). The expansion accelerates exactly when \(w < -\tfrac13\).

Radiation's 1/3 comes straight from kinetic theory. A photon carries momentum \(p = E/c\), and an isotropic gas of particles exerts pressure \(\tfrac13\,n\,p\,v\), so \(P = \tfrac13\rho c^2\). It checks out independently: the number of photons dilutes as \(a^{-3}\) and each one's energy is redshifted as \(a^{-1}\), giving \(a^{-4}\), just as (10) says.

A cosmological constant is the opposite extreme. Its density does not dilute at all, so the fluid equation forces \(\rho + P/c^2 = 0\), and \(w = -1\). That is a negative pressure, a tension. Stretching a box of it creates energy, at exactly the rate \(-P\,dV\) supplies.

Every curve passes through \(a = 1\) today with the same slope, because they share today's \(H_0\). They differ in the past. The more negative \(w\) is, the more slowly the universe has been slowing down, and the further back the big bang sits: \(2/3(1+w)\) Hubble times ago. At \(w = -1\) there is no big bang at all, and the curve runs back forever.

The universe's own \(w\)

The real universe is a mixture, and each ingredient dilutes at its own rate (10). Whichever has the most negative \(w\) wins in the end. The equation of state of the whole universe is the energy-weighted average, and it sets the deceleration parameter \(q \equiv -\ddot a a/\dot a^2\):

\[ w_\mathrm{eff}(a) = \frac{\sum_i w_i\,\rho_i(a)}{\sum_i \rho_i(a)}, \qquad q = \tfrac12\,(1 + 3\,w_\mathrm{eff}) . \tag{12} \]

Today's budget is \(\Omega_\mathrm{m} = 0.315\) in matter, \(\Omega_\mathrm{r} \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-5}\) in photons and neutrinos, and the rest, about 0.685, in dark energy. The Friedmann equation becomes

\[ \frac{H^2(a)}{H_0^2} = \Omega_\mathrm{r}\,a^{-4} + \Omega_\mathrm{m}\,a^{-3} + \Omega_\mathrm{DE}\,f_\mathrm{DE}(a) , \tag{13} \]

with \(f_\mathrm{DE} = 1\) for a cosmological constant. If dark energy is not a constant, the standard way to let it evolve is \(w(a) = w_0 + w_a(1 - a)\). Putting that into the fluid equation and integrating gives

\[ f_\mathrm{DE}(a) = a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)}\, e^{-3w_a(1-a)} . \]

That is the model DESI fits. Combined with the microwave background and supernovae, DESI's second data release prefers \(w_0 > -1\) and \(w_a < 0\) over a cosmological constant, at between about 2.8 and \(4.2\sigma\) depending on which supernova sample is used. The panel below has both.

Read the lower panel from left to right. The universe began radiation-dominated, with \(w_\mathrm{eff} = \tfrac13\). Matter overtook radiation at a redshift of about 3400, some fifty thousand years in, and \(w_\mathrm{eff}\) sank to nearly zero, where it stayed for most of cosmic history while galaxies formed. Only about six billion years ago did it cross \(-\tfrac13\), and the expansion began to speed up. With a cosmological constant it now heads steadily toward −1 and exponential expansion.

Press the DESI button and the history changes in an interesting way. The dark energy is phantom in the past, with \(w < -1\), and has drifted above −1 in the last few billion years. It is less dense early on and more important late, which is what shifts the measured distances. The far right of the panel is the formula extrapolated well past the data, so take the future it shows as illustration rather than prediction.

The Newtonian derivation shows that the expansion law is ordinary physics: a self-gravitating ball, thrown outward. The equation of state is where Newton runs out. Once pressure is a source of gravity, a negative enough pressure makes gravity push, and that is the simplest way to see why an accelerating universe needs something with \(w < -\tfrac13\). Whether that something is a constant, as Einstein's \(\Lambda\) would be, or slowly changing, as the DESI data hint, is now a measurement.
