The expansion has no center

Click any star. It stays where it is, and the whole sky moves away from it — then click another one and that becomes the center instead

Two copies of the same photograph are stacked here. The white one is the sky as it is; the orange one is the same sky uniformly expanded, every separation multiplied by the same factor. Nothing is added, moved by hand, or singled out. Click any star and the expanded copy is scaled about that star, so the two copies agree there and disagree everywhere else. That star sits still. Everything else slides outward, and the further away it started, the further it goes.

7%  

Infrared image: a 4° field at RA 269.730°, Dec +66.020°, from the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys viewer, unWISE/NEOWISE layer, 19.3″ per pixel. The four marked stars are the brightest sources in each quadrant of the frame. The idea for this demonstration is Viktor T. Toth's — why cosmic expansion has no center — which uses a synthetic star field and asks you to drag one copy into alignment. Here the alignment is done for you, exactly, about whichever star you click.

Why every star looks like the center

Expansion multiplies every separation by the same factor. If two stars were d apart and the universe grows by 7%, they are now 1.07d apart, so they separated by 0.07d. A star twice as far away separated twice as much. That is Hubble's law, and it is what you are watching: the orange counterparts near your chosen star barely move, and the ones at the edge of the frame move a long way.

The point is what happens when you click a different star. Nothing about the photograph changed — the same uniform expansion, the same factor — yet the new star now sits still while the old one races away. Both descriptions are correct simultaneously, which is only possible if neither star is special. An observer on any of them would measure everything receding, and would be entitled to think themselves at the center. That is why the question where did the Big Bang happen has no answer of the form over there: it happened everywhere, and every observer sees the recession from their own vantage.

What the demonstration is not: these stars are in our own galaxy, and they are not receding from each other. The Milky Way is held together, and so are galaxy clusters; expansion shows up only on scales large enough that gravity has not won locally. This is a picture of the geometry, using a real sky because a real sky is more convincing than a scatter of dots — not a claim about these particular stars. For the actual measurement of the expansion at many redshifts, see baryon acoustic oscillations.
